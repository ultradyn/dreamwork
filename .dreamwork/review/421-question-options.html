The four options — each with what it reduces, what it costs, and its risk
optionreducescostsrisk
A · the ask comes first, in its own line Time to find the decision. Not length — the entry stays the same size, the decision just rises to the top of it. One convention. lint can check that the first body line carries an Ask: marker. An Ask: line that merely restates the title adds a line and no information. The check must require it to name the accepted answers, not merely exist.
B · an unanswered sub-decision is recorded, and lint errors The 2 durable partials in the corpus, and — more usefully — the recurrence rather than the instances. A format rule in file-formats.md plus a lint ERROR. Composes with #419's blocked-on-human marker (same shape, one door over) rather than duplicating it. The only option here with a real implementation cost, and it does nothing about length.
C · a length budget, evidence in the artifact Length directly. Corpus median 308 → ≤250; max 1252 → capped. A lint warning on over-budget entries; more weight on review artifacts being opened. Load-bearing. An artifact he does not open makes the evidence less visible than a long entry does. Today's #263 correction lived in the entry and he could read it without a click — a cap would have pushed it behind one. This is the argument against the coordinator's own tidiest idea, so C is left soft.
D · state what a valid answer looks like The cost of answering, not of reading. Nearly nothing. A stated answer set that omits the option he actually wants is worse than none, so it must always admit a free-text answer explicitly — as the ask at the top of this page does.

Rejected, and the number that kills it
One decision per entry. The obvious port from i-have-adhd's one-clarifying-question rule, and our own data refutes it: 15 of 16 answered multi-part entries closed complete — often same-day, often on a bare rec. Durable partials number 2 across the whole corpus. Splitting every multi-part ask would multiply the count of items on his desk to solve a problem occurring 2 of 58 times. Recorded as rejected with its reason so it is not re-proposed.
The measurement that carries the argument

One comparison is more persuasive than the whole distribution, because it is counter-intuitive and one number makes it: the two entries whose titles promise a one-word answer — “one word: may I add GIT_OPTIONAL_LOCKS=0…” and “one word: may I run install.py --apply?” — are 307 and 455 words. The corpus median is 308. So a one-word decision gets a median-length essay (or a half-again-longer one). The size of what we write is weakly coupled to the size of what we are asking — and that, not the number of sub-questions, is the defect the corpus actually shows.

Every figure above was re-derived from .dreamwork/questions.md with the loop's own production parsers, in the command the page is bound to:

python3 -c "
import watch, statistics
from pathlib import Path
t = Path('.dreamwork/questions.md').read_text()
q = watch.parse_open_questions(t) + watch.parse_answered(t)
w = [len((it['title'] or '')+'\n'+(it.get('body') or '')).split()) for it in q]
print('n', len(q), 'median', int(statistics.median(w)),
      'min', min(w), 'max', max(w))
"

Sub-decisions per entry are derived with the corpus grammar from the research (**Q1 — / numbered 1. ** markers, falling back to a single decision if the body carries a ?). 15 / 16 is hand-verified against each answered multi-part entry's fold head: one (#281) closed six-of-seven with a re-ask, the rest closed as complete rulings.
