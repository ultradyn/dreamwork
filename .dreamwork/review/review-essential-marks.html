The shape: one behaviour, two presentations, drawn to scale

A flag marks a height, the way a physical one does — it protrudes at the height of the clause regardless of what is printed on that line. So it anchors to the reading column's right edge and takes its vertical position from the passage, which is what makes it immune to the block-width problem that killed the third design.

≥780px — the flag rail1:3.2 — every x is a real measurement
<780px — the essentials strip1:2 — the tab that does not fit
Why this counts as one feature and not two. Next/prev walks the marks in document order in both presentations, and both derive from the same source-declared list. The 780px switch changes where a mark is drawn and nothing about what a mark is or what pressing next does. If the two forms needed different behaviour, that would be the signal the design was wrong.
Marks come from the source, and that is half the value

The authoring dreamer declares them, and the cap is five. An artifact that cannot name its three-to-five essential passages has not decided what it is asking you for — so the cap is a forcing function on the writer before it is a convenience for the reader. That is worth as much as your saved minutes, and it is the reason not to derive marks automatically: a heuristic would produce twenty-two of them and mean nothing.

Four decisions, with recommendations
M1 · The rail above 780px, the strip below it — two presentations of one behaviour, switching at the measured cliff.
Rec: take it. The alternative is one presentation everywhere, which means either no flags on a narrow window or a strip on a 1440px monitor with 506px of wrap sitting empty beside the text. Both are worse in a way you would notice within a day.
M2 · Cap of five, and over the cap refuses the build — rather than warning, or a higher number.
Rec: five, refusing. Your own framing is that flags work because there are few of them; the artifact that needs them most has 22 sections, so the pressure to creep is real and immediate. Say a number if five is wrong — it is one constant and trivial to change — but the refusing half is what makes it hold.
M3 · How long is a label, and who shortens it? A tab is ~96px, which is roughly 12 characters. Either the author writes short and the builder refuses anything longer, or the builder truncates.
Rec: refuse, do not truncate. Truncation is silent and produces "the transition boun…" on your page; refusal happens on my machine while the author is still there to write a better label. This is the one decision where I would like your read rather than your ratification — you are the person who reads the tabs.
M4 · Do marks also appear in the top-rail nav?
Rec: no. The nav is the structural axis (findings / shape / decision); marks are the "read-this-if-nothing-else" axis. Merging them gives you the table of contents this design spent its first measurement ruling out. The cost of being wrong here is small and reversible, which is why it is last.
What approval means, and what it does not
Approving accepts the four decisions above and nothing more. It does not authorise touching the template, restamping the 16 artifacts, adding a class to any existing source, changing the nav, or shipping a keybinding — the first commit is the source contract in file-formats.md plus the "no marks → byte-identical" check, red first, and it changes no artifact you have read. The frame change is a separate increment with a separate rebuild, deliberately, because template_stamp digests the frame's bytes and every artifact restamps at once.
