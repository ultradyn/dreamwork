The invariant, not the order, is what keeps this safe: his words never have fewer durable homes than at the previous commit. Two different things get called "shadow" and they point in opposite directions, so the plan names them separately — the journal-shadow phase before the cutover, where the journal is written and its failures swallowed, and the log-shadow phase after it, where submissions.log is best-effort by your decision 3.
Journal-shadow. A receipt is committed on every write while the response, the log and every handler stay byte-identical to a captured baseline. Rolling back is a revert.
Cutover. The journal commit authorises the response. Preconditions: the two-process test green, a real-target shadow window with the two counts asserted at runtime, and all fifteen 200 assertions moving in the same commit.
Log-shadow. A submissions.log failure becomes health, not a refusal — and a one-line grep in the CLI's own tests keeps the log from quietly becoming load-bearing again.

What the plan does about fixture 3, and why it does not fudge it. Increment 22 tests the contract — any journal open-or-commit failure yields no 202 — at a real seam: the journal's parent directory chmod 0500 before start, so the open genuinely fails and nothing is mocked. The fsync-specific case is recorded as a deferred gap with the mechanism named (an LD_PRELOAD shim failing fsync for one path). Your own design says crash fixtures must "kill at named seams rather than mocking away durability" — so a mocked fsync is the exact thing that sentence forbids, and leaving the gap visible is the design-conformant answer rather than the lazy one.

The plan also names, per increment, what its tests must not fake — because the failure mode here is not a missing test, it is a thorough one that cannot fail. Four of the traps it forbids by name:

threads instead of processes passes with no DB constraint at all
